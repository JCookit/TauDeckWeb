## Living in Harmony

By this point, it’s fair to ask whether this is just too much.

There are a lot of systems on these cards. Enough that it would be easy for them to compete with each other, or for the whole thing to feel noisy and hard to use.

I don’t think that happens in practice, but it’s not a given. It’s something I had to pay attention to along the way.

---

## Visual Hierarchy

The biggest factor is visual hierarchy.

Not everything is trying to be seen at once.

The corners carry the primary identity—suit and rank, the things you need immediately if you’re using the deck like a normal deck. The center is reserved for the icon and the word, which is where most of the narrative weight sits.

Everything else is pushed to the edges or reduced in emphasis.

Color, weight, font, shape, and placement are all doing work here. Some elements are bold and high contrast, others are deliberately quiet. The goal is that you can glance at a card and only see what you need.

And if you use the deck repeatedly, you start to learn where things are. Humans are generally good at this. We get used to visual systems and filter them without thinking about it. What looks dense at first becomes readable with a bit of exposure.

---

## Optional Layers

That ties into a second point, which is that most systems are optional in use as well.

You don’t have to engage with all of them at once.

You can play something that only cares about suits and ranks and it behaves like a standard deck. You can use just the icons and play a matching game. You can use the letters for word games. You can use borders and treat it like a simple spatial system.

The rest of the structure is there if you want it, but it doesn’t get in the way if you don’t.

---

## Different Jobs for Different Systems

Another thing that helps is that the systems don’t overlap much in what they’re trying to do.

Suits and ranks handle grouping and ordering. Spheres and modes provide alternate partitions. Borders are spatial. Links are relational. The scoring systems are numeric.

They sit beside each other rather than on top of each other.

So when you combine them, you’re not resolving conflicts so much as adding constraints.

---

## Useful Redundancy

There is some redundancy, but it’s deliberate.

Rank has both a number and a glyph. Borders have both edge values and a total. Links show both direction and how connected a card is.

That might look like duplication, but it’s doing what it’s supposed to do—giving you multiple ways to access the same information. If you want to frame it in Tufte terms, the goal was to make every element do more than one job. There isn’t much on the card that is purely decorative.

---

## Balance Beneath the Surface

The other part is less visible, but probably more important.

A lot of effort went into keeping the distributions balanced.

Icon pairs don’t share suits or ranks. Partitions have equal color spread and mode coverage. Border configurations and scores follow patterns. Letter values are shaped to behave predictably.

You don’t see that on a single card, but you feel it when you start using the deck. Combinations behave in a way that makes sense more often than not.

---

## Returning to the Original Goals

Stepping back, this ties into the original goals.

One was that you could map existing games onto the deck, at least in a loose sense. That turns out to be a spectrum.

At one end, you can get very close to a standard deck. Filter down to a 4-suit subset and use ranks normally, and most familiar games work with minimal adjustment.

Slightly further along, you get games like Uno. You’re not reproducing it exactly, but you can filter the deck to the size you need and decide which ranks or glyphs represent the action cards. That’s still a fairly light mapping.

Further still are more modern small-deck games—things like Love Letter, Skull, or other compact designs. Those don’t map one-to-one, but the process is similar: select a subset of cards and assign roles based on the systems that are already there.

At that point, you’re not porting a game so much as rebuilding it using the deck as a base.

---

Another goal was that there would be enough variety to support new games.

That mostly comes from combining systems—using constraints that don’t exist in standard decks. Things like layering partitions with ranks, or combining links with scoring, or using borders for placement while still caring about suit or mode.

And the third goal was that it could function as something more open-ended and entertaining.

The words, icons, and overlays support interpretation, narrative, inspiration or prompt-based use without needing additional structure.

---

## Who the Deck Is For

All of that said, it still might not work for everyone.

This isn’t a minimal deck. It’s closer to something you explore over time, or something you build on. People who like puzzles, systems, or figuring out how pieces fit together will probably get more out of it than someone looking for a quick familiar experience.

And that’s fine.

---

The goal wasn’t to make something universal in the sense that everyone would immediately use it the same way.

It was to make something that holds together, even with a lot going on, and gives you room to decide how much of it you want to engage with.
