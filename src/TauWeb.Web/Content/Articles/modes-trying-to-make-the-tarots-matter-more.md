## Echo of an Idea

Modes had a longer path than most of the other systems.

The starting point was pretty simple. I had the Tarots, and I liked them as anchors in the deck. They carry a lot of weight, both thematically and historically.

By then, the deck already had suits and ranks providing structure, spheres acting as a clean partition, and icons and words carrying most of the meaning. So the question wasn’t just “what else can I add?”

It was whether the Tarots could matter *beyond just being individual cards*?

That led to an early idea I called “echoes.”

Each Tarot would have a small group of related cards—roughly six total. One Tarot, plus five others that shared something with it. It worked reasonably well in early passes. It gave you a narrative cluster, something you could build around. And visually, I created a set of simple line-drawn glyphs—geometric shapes that loosely referenced each Tarot in the group.

So now I had 25 groups of 6.

## Two Grids That Needed to Connect

Around the same time, I reworked spheres into 6 groups of 25.

And that’s when I realized I had built two completely different partition systems:

- 6 groups of 25 (spheres)
- 25 groups of 6 (echoes)

…and they had nothing to do with each other.

That didn’t sit right.

Not because either system was bad, but because they were **independent in the wrong way**.

They didn’t interfere—but they also didn’t interact, which means you couldn’t really use them together.

It’s just more structure for the sake of structure.

---

## Making the Groups Intersect

So the question became:  can these be merged without losing what makes each useful?

The six spheres felt too clean to give up.  They divide the deck evenly, they’re easy to reason about, and they already align with other systems. So the work shifted to reshaping the echoes so that they would fit that structure.

The restrictions I ended up with was:

- each group has 6 cards (unchanged)
- one of those 6 is a Tarot (unchanged)
- one from each sphere (new!)

If that works, then you get a nice symmetry:

- each sphere is made up of 25 groups
- each group spans all spheres

That’s a clean orthogonal cut across the deck. every grouping now intersects every other grouping in a predictable way.  That’s where it becomes usable.

---

## Finding the Emotional Layer

The next question was: what are these 25 groups?

What’s the “something” that ties each set of 6 cards together in a meaningful way?

I went through a few variations.

I went through a few variations—some abstract “color feeling,” some notion of scale, generic adjectives—but none of it really stuck.   What did work was emotional state.

It’s broad enough to apply to almost any card, but still specific enough to be meaningful. And it fits the tone that was already emerging from the words.

So the “echoes” became “modes” -- a set of 25 emotional or experiential states that every card could be mapped into.  Some of them fit really naturally. Things like **Confusion** or **Connection** almost assign themselves. Others took more iteration, but overall it held together.   That looseness was important.

Too rigid and the assignments feel forced. Too vague and the groups stop being useful.

It also gave a bit of structure within the 25. You can group them, order them, and use them to tell a loose story.

## Designing the Mode Glyphs

Visually, this is where things got more experimental.

The earlier “echo” glyphs were tied to Tarots and were more literal – stylized interpretations of those words – the Emperor echo was a basic crown for example. Once this became a more abstract emotional system, I needed a different direction.   Also, I was pretty sure these glyphs would live next to spheres, which already carried color and abstract shape.

So they needed to:

- read at small scale
- nave a distinct silhouette
- not compete visually
- capable of carrying some color

And then a few additional constraints:

- should encode some kind of number or structure if possible
- should have a logical system behind them
- should **not** have rotational symmetry (so upside-down doesn’t read as something valid)

---

## A Triangle-Based Alphabet

The triangle-based system came out of that.

Once I started working with triangles and their arrangements, it was possible to build a consistent set that met all those constraints. The shapes are distinct, they read well at small size, and they can carry color without becoming noisy.

And like the spheres, they ended up encoding a number system as well.  (from 0-24, although the system extends beyond that).   I want to make symbols work hard for the card real estate, so it should do more than one job.

For placing in the card, now i had two glyphs that worked together in a way separate from other systems (that could each carry unrelated color).  Obviously, they should sit together (which wasn't necessary true for the first 'echo' idea).   Pairing them top-right under the sequence number was the natural position.

---

At this point, modes felt like a real system.

They replaced the earlier “echoes,” but kept the original goal—connecting cards in a way that reinforces the Tarots and provides another usable partition of the deck.

The difference is that now they actually align with the rest of the structure, instead of sitting off to the side.

They’re not perfect. The emotional mapping is somewhat interpretive, some assignments are stronger than others, and the numbering is easy to ignore.

But they hold together, and they interact cleanly with every other system in the deck.

That’s what justifies them.

## For players & designers:

The simplest use of modes is just as another partition.

You can deal cards and ask for sets of six, one from each sphere, which also means one from each mode group. Because of how they’re constructed, those sets are always balanced in a way that the other systems aren’t.

It’s also an easy constraint to layer on top of existing mechanics. Instead of collecting runs or matching suits, you might require a spread of different modes, or avoid duplicates. It changes the feel of otherwise familiar games without needing to introduce anything new.

Where it gets more interesting is that each group is anchored by a Tarot.

That gives you a natural “center” to the set. If you’re building or collecting, you can treat the Tarot as something special, or as a requirement, or even as something to avoid. It creates asymmetry in a way that still feels structured.

For more open-ended use, modes probably do the most work.

They give you a built-in emotional layer that sits on top of whatever the card already is. You don’t have to invent that interpretation—it’s already there. If you’re doing any kind of prompt-based or narrative use, that becomes a strong signal very quickly.

And because the groups cut across suits, ranks, and spheres, you end up with combinations that don’t really show up anywhere else in the deck. That’s where a lot of the more novel uses come from.

And remember modes are actually numbered, and grouped into color-families, so it is also possible to compare, sort and order them.

Even if you ignore everything else, just working within those 25 groups is enough to build something interesting.
