## A Brand New Sunday Suit

The idea of having 10 suits was there from the start.

Partly because it’s just a nice number to work with. But more importantly, it naturally gives you five pairs—and that maps really cleanly to five base colors you can actually reuse:

black, red, blue, yellow, green.

---

## The Familiar Four

Then, standard playing cards give you four suits for free.

Spades, hearts, clubs, diamonds. Everyone already knows them, and more importantly, they already *work*. They’re readable at a glance, they behave well in the corners, and they carry just enough identity without getting in the way.

Expanding from four to ten means you don’t get to lean on that anymore.

You have to invent six more that don’t feel like second-class citizens.

---

## Naming the Suits

Coming up with some preliminary names was next, and that had its own constraints to “feel right”.

Suit names had to be:

- reasonably universal (and fit what was emerging as the “mythic tone” of the deck)
- easy to understand
- flexible enough to map across the different word domains that were established

They don’t need to be perfect. They just need to not get in the way.

You should be able to say them out loud, use them in a game, or reinterpret them if you want. “Clubs” becoming “Clovers” is fine. That kind of flexibility is actually a feature.

And I had to be able to draw them.

This is where I gained a lot more appreciation for standard playing cards.

Those shapes are doing a lot of work.

They are:

- simple enough to be readable at very small sizes
- distinct from each other, even without color
- recognizable at a glance
- vertically symmetrical, which matters more than I expected
- loosely tied to their names, but still with character
- and grouped in pairs that share some visual trait

---

## Hard and Soft Pairs

Another idea I borrowed from EverDeck was this notion of pairing “hard” and “soft” suits.

The traditional suits kind of do this already.

Spades and clubs feel sharper, more structural.Hearts and diamonds feel softer, more rounded.

It’s not a strict rule, but it’s there, so can I extend that idea across ten suits? (It would create a natural deck bifurcation that could be leveraged).

That gave me a direction for the six new suits—each color pair could split into a sharper and a softer form.

---

## Arriving at the Suit Set

The end result was the current set.

Some of them came together quickly. Others didn’t.

Leaves went through quite a few iterations before landing on something that felt right—simple enough, but still distinct. Crescents were pushed toward a rotated, more symmetrical, almost stylized shape to keep that vertical balance and give them a slightly more “mythic” feel.

Flames ended up looking a bit like drops, depending on how you look at them.

At this point, I’m okay with a little ambiguity, as long as the shape is stable and recognizable.

---

## Choosing Usable Colors

Color was a separate problem.

Even though I had the five base colors in mind early, actually picking usable shades took some iteration, especially given the earlier constraint that color couldn’t dominate the card.

They needed to:

- reproduce well in print
- have roughly similar brightness so one suit doesn’t dominate
- work both as fills and as text
- and still feel distinct from each other

This is another place where AI helped—not to decide *what* the colors should be, but to sanity check combinations and catch cases where something looked fine in isolation but didn’t hold up across the full set.

Yellow was the main challenge.

Pure yellow tends to disappear, especially when used for text or fine detail. So that shifted more toward a gold tone—still clearly “yellow,” but with enough weight to hold up on the card.

---

At the end of this, suits finally felt real.

Not just categories in a spreadsheet, but something you could actually see and use.

They had shape, color, and behaved well in the parts of the card that needed to be instantly readable.

Which is important, because everything else is about to start piling on top of them.

## For players & designers:

At the simplest level, suits behave exactly like a standard deck.

You can use them for matching, trick-taking, or any familiar mechanic without needing to reinterpret anything. Red and black groupings still work naturally, and with ten suits it’s also easy to treat the deck as two parallel sets if needed.

The hard and soft pairing adds a small extension.

Each color has two related suits, which gives you a built-in way to split or group them further. You can treat them as equivalent, or require one of each within a color, depending on what the game needs.

Beyond that, suits mostly act as a stable partition.

They give you ten evenly sized groups that can be used on their own or combined with other systems—runs within a suit, sets across suits, or constraints layered with ranks or spheres.

There is also a light thematic use.

Each suit carries a general tone, so it’s possible to treat it as a lens on the card’s meaning if you want to. But that’s secondary—the primary role is structural.
