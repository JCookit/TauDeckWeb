## Themes, Bands, and Glyphs Oh My

Ranks already existed in the deck from the start.

They had ordering, and they had themes that were emerging from the words. That part was working.

But I also knew ranks were going to get reused for gameplay.

\[note: can insert something about the rank overloads – A, J, Q, K and the specials X, Y, Z\]

If someone is playing a standard game or something Uno-like, it’s very natural to say “everything above 10 does something special.” Draw, reverse, skip—those kinds of mechanics.

So it felt like ranks should carry that weight by default.

---

## Giving Each Rank an Action

That led to the idea that each rank should have an associated **action**.

Not in a prescriptive way—the deck isn’t trying to tell you how to play—but as a default. Something that makes it easy to pick up and say “ok, these cards probably do this.”

In an early version, I actually printed those action words directly on the card.

That didn’t last, but the idea stuck.

---

So now each rank had a few things tied together:

- a theme (coming from the words)
- a potential gameplay action
- and something visual to represent that

The number alone wasn’t quite enough.

You *can* remember that “14 means reverse” or whatever you decide, but rank is such a central mechanic in most card games that it felt like it deserved a symbol as well.

Some people will use the number. Some will use the symbol. Either way works.

---

The goal then was to connect those pieces in a way that felt natural.

For example:

Rank 7 is themed around **Motion**.That maps pretty cleanly to an action like **Move**.Which suggests something like an arrow for the glyph.

You don’t have to use it that way. But it gives you a starting point.

---

## Designing Simple Glyphs

For the glyphs themselves, I wanted them to be extremely simple.

They live in a pretty important place—right below the suit, duplicated in both corners—so they’re part of the first thing you see when you look at a card.

If they’re too detailed or too flashy, they become distracting very quickly.

So the approach was to borrow from things people already recognize.

Punctuation turned out to be a surprisingly good source.

- **Command** → !
- **Choose** → ?

Those just work. No explanation needed.

From there it expanded a bit:

- an arrow for **Move**
- an infinity symbol for **Start** (“infinite possibilities”)

Simple shapes that already carry meaning.

---

## Patterns That Emerged

There were a couple of happy accidents along the way.

After fitting in a square and a circle (Ground and Form), I realized I could add a triangle and a two-bar pause symbol (Push and Join).

That set of four ended up looking exactly like old VCR controls.

Not intentional, but kind of perfect.

---

## Mathematical Operators

The other one was the math operators.

+−\*/=

They fit surprisingly well with the rank themes:

- Forge
- Shift
- Reveal
- Craft
- Reflect

And they suggest some obvious gameplay uses—math games, quick calculations, that sort of thing.

That one I was pretty happy about.

---

## Hard and Soft Shapes

Visually, I also wanted to echo the “hard / soft” idea from the suits.

So each glyph has two forms—one more rectangular, one more rounded.

It’s subtle, but it reinforces that pairing idea across the deck.

---

These settled into white glyphs on a solid background.

They’re easy to spot, easy to scan in a hand, and they don’t compete too much with the icon or the word.

---

## Finding a Role for Color

Color for ranks went through its own iteration.

My first instinct was to run a full rainbow across the ranks—red through orange through yellow, all the way to purple.

Some decks do this and it looks nice in isolation.

But in practice, it was too much.

There’s already a lot of color coming from suits. Adding a full spectrum on top of that just made everything feel noisy.

---

I still wanted color to be part of rank identity, though.

The compromise was to group ranks into three bands:

- orange
- teal
- purple

These sit apart from the suit colors, so they don’t compete directly, and they give a quick visual segmentation of the deck into thirds.  (These are also great thematic colors for Beginnings, Journey and Endings which align with these 5 rank groups fairly well)

Within each band, the individual ranks get slight variations in shade.

---

So at this point, ranks had:

- a number
- a theme
- a potential action
- a glyph
- and a color band

Which sounds like a lot, but it ended up working because each piece is doing a slightly different job.

And like everything else, the goal wasn’t to force usage.

It was to make the information available in a way that’s easy to pick up when you want it, and easy to ignore when you don’t.

## For players & designers:

At the most basic level, ranks behave exactly how you expect.

You can use them as straight ordering—high card, runs, comparisons—and everything works the same way as a standard deck. Nothing special required.

The added layer is the actions.

Because each rank has a suggested action, it’s easy to map higher ranks to special behavior. Anything unused can be treated as “event” cards—draw, reverse, skip, etc.—without needing to invent that mapping from scratch.

The glyphs make that easier to remember.

Instead of “14 means reverse,” you can just look for the symbol. In practice, some people will use the numbers, some the glyphs, and both work interchangeably.   Some glyphs (like the math operations) might suggest specific kinds of games.

The color bands add another layer.

Since the ranks are grouped into thirds, you can segment the deck quickly. Low, mid, high. Or beginnings, transitions, endings. That shows up naturally in gameplay—drafting, progression, or any kind of staged mechanic.

Those bands can also be used independently of the exact rank. If a game only cares about “early vs late” or “weak vs strong,” the color does that without needing to track exact values.

You can also combine these layers.

For example, you might trigger actions only on certain bands, or require specific glyph types within a band. Because the systems are aligned, those combinations are predictable and easy to reason about.

Even if you ignore the themes entirely, just using rank + glyph + band gives you a flexible set of mechanics that behave like a standard deck, but with more options already built in.
