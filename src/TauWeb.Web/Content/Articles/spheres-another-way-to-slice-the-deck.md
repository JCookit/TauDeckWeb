## Fire for Fire, Water for Water

One of the first novel additions came from leaning into the “mystical” side of things a bit more.

Up to this point, most of the systems were pretty grounded—ranks, suits, icons, letters. All very usable, very mechanical. But the tone of the words was already drifting in a more mythic direction, and it felt like there was room for another grouping that could be used both thematically and for gameplay.

At this point I already had:

- suits as a primary grouping
- ranks as structure
- icons and words tying things together

So the question wasn’t whether another grouping was possible.

It was whether it could exist **without overlapping what was already there**.

The four classical elements were the obvious starting point.

Fire, water, air, earth. Everyone already understands them, and they map surprisingly well onto a wide range of concepts. A first pass through the word list suggested that I could assign most cards without forcing it too much, which is always a good sign.

But four didn’t quite work.

150 doesn’t divide cleanly by 4, and more importantly, it didn’t feel like enough separation. So I added a fifth category—at first this was something like “cosmos,” just to round things out.

That helped with coverage, but it raised a different question.

---

## Do I actually need this?

The deck already had a strong five-way division through the suit colors and their themes. Adding another five-way split felt redundant.

It might work, but it wasn’t obviously adding anything new.   This couldn’t just be “another way to group things”; it had to be a different kind of grouping, and it had to pull its weight in functionality.

I started looking at the numbers a bit more.

150 is also 6 × 25.

That’s a much cleaner partition if you want evenly sized groups. So instead of forcing five, it made more sense to move to six.

That solved two things at once:

- clean distribution
- clear separation from the existing five-color system

The four elements stayed. The fifth slot shifted, and the sixth became its opposite.

That’s how it ended up as:

- Fire / Force
- Water / State
- Air / Mind
- Earth / Matter
- Order / Pattern
- Chaos / Mystery

The element names also needed to stretch a bit. Pure “fire” or “water” is too narrow if you’re trying to map a large set of abstract words. So each one picked up a second term to widen the interpretation. Fire encompasses aggressive force or energy. Water includes emotional state. That kind of thing.

That looseness was important.

If the categories are too strict, you spend all your time forcing assignments. If they’re too loose, they stop meaning anything.

This is somewhere in the middle.

---

## Designing the Sphere Glyphs

Visually, I wanted something simple again.   Importantly, this couldn’t compete with suits or rank glyphs.   It needed to be:  recognizable, compact, and ignorable if unused.

I kept coming back to the circle.   (Actually the name “spheres” and the usage of a circle as a glyph happened around the same time; I’m not sure which was first!)

Circles are clean, it’s neutral, and it has a nice symmetry to it. There’s also a bit of a nod to classical alchemical symbols, or even something like *The Fifth Element*, where those designs have a very simple, almost geometric identity.

The breakthrough was realizing that if add internal lines to the circle, things could form a pattern.  The intersections of these lines with the containing circle naturally created a count.

So instead of just having six distinct shapes, each glyph also creates a number of intersection nodes - which became a value from 0 to 5.

That wasn’t the primary goal, but it’s the kind of dual use I like—one symbol doing two jobs without adding extra clutter.   If you just need a grouping, look at the shapes.  If you need a number for sorting or value, count the dots.   There was a tension to balancing the visual weight and making it recognizable and useful.   In the end I selected a little bit of brightness (I like a little pop) but balanced with muted fill, appropriate size and line thickness.

---

## A Useful Second Grouping

At that point, spheres had a role.

They provided another way to group the deck that wasn’t tied to suits or ranks, and they introduced a slightly more abstract layer that fit the tone of the words.

More importantly, they don’t replace anything and they sit cleanly alongside everything else.

They’re not perfect.

- the categories are a little interpretive
- some assignments are better fits than others
- the numeric layer is easy to ignore

But that’s acceptable; they hold together as a system, and provide value, and they don’t interfere with the rest of the deck.

## For players & designers:

The most straightforward use for spheres is just as a clean partition.

Because the deck is evenly split into 6 groups of 25, you can ask for “one of each sphere” and know that it behaves predictably no matter how the cards are dealt.

It’s also easy to layer on top of existing mechanics. Instead of collecting runs or matching suits, you might collect across spheres, or require a spread of different ones. It gives you a different constraint without changing much else.

Where it starts to get more useful is when you combine it with other systems.

Since spheres cut across suits and ranks, you can do things like:

one of each sphere within a suit

or one of each sphere across different ranks

There’s also a bit of interpretation available if you want it. The pairings (Order / Chaos, Mind / Matter, etc.) give you a way to read balance or tension across a set without needing to define anything extra.

And like the glyphs suggest, there is a quiet numeric layer there as well. You can ignore it entirely, or use it for ordering or comparison if that’s useful.
