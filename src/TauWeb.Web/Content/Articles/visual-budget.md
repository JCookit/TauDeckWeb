## Creating an Eye Chart

Up to this point, most of this lived in a spreadsheet and scattered SVG files.

Which is a very forgiving place to design something like this.

You can move things around freely. Swap words. Rebalance distributions. Try different groupings. Nothing really pushes back. If something doesn’t work, you just change it and move on.

A card is not like that.

---

By now I at least knew the main pieces I was dealing with. Each card was going to have a word, a suit, a rank, and eventually an icon. And realistically, I already knew there would be more layered in later.

Even with just those pieces, it was enough to start asking a different kind of question. Not whether the system worked in theory, but whether it could actually exist in a physical form without collapsing under its own weight.

That’s a different kind of thinking.

Up to this point it had mostly been about structure—getting the words to behave, getting suits and ranks to feel coherent, making sure nothing obviously broke. Here, the design side of the brain starts to kick in, even if you don’t quite realize it yet. You start thinking in terms of space, and attention, and what can realistically coexist on a single card.

You don’t formalize it. It’s more of a creeping awareness.

Some things feel like they want to be central. Others feel like they could fade into the background. And even before anything is fully drawn, you can sense that if everything demands equal attention, the whole thing is going to fall apart.

---

## Rough Mockups

So I started making rough mockups.

Not designs. Just placements.

Where does the word go? Where does rank live? How much room does a suit actually need? If an icon is going to be present, how big can it be before everything else starts to shrink around it?

You sketch something out, look at it, and you don’t get a clear answer so much as a reaction. Some arrangements feel workable. Others feel crowded in ways that are hard to articulate, but easy to recognize.

Nothing was really “failing,” but there was definitely friction.

---

## Budgeting Attention

One thing that became clearer through that process is that space isn’t the only constraint—attention is just as important.

You can technically fit quite a lot on a card. But that doesn’t mean a person can *read* it, or understand it quickly, or even know where to look first. Some elements naturally pull focus, even when they’re small. Others disappear unless you deliberately give them weight.

That balance wasn’t solved here, but it was clearly going to matter.

---

## When Elements Compete

There’s also the interaction between elements, which doesn’t really show up until you start putting things next to each other.

Individually, everything made sense. Word, suit, rank—each one had a reason to exist. But once they shared the same space, you started to see where things competed, or overlapped, or just felt a little off. Spacing that seemed fine in isolation suddenly felt tight. Relationships that were clean in a spreadsheet started to blur visually.

Again, not broken. Just not clean.

---

## Finding the Visual Budget

Somewhere in here, I realized I had something like a “visual budget.”

Not in a precise sense, but enough to reason about.

I knew roughly how large the icon could be—on the order of an inch and a half square if it was going to carry any real detail. I had a sense of how big rank indicators needed to be from looking at standard playing cards, and how small they could get before they stopped being usable. Glyphs and secondary elements had a range where they were visible without taking over.

Put all of that together, and you get a kind of mental accounting.

If the icon takes this much space, and the word needs this much room to breathe, and rank and suit need to be readable at a glance, what’s left?

It wasn’t exact, but it was enough to answer the important question.

---

Which was: is this even feasible?

And at this point, the answer seemed to be yes.

Busy, maybe. But not impossible.

There was still room to maneuver. Nothing was obviously overcrowded beyond recovery, and nothing essential had to be thrown out just to make the card readable.

That’s about as much as you can hope for at this stage.

---

So the goal here wasn’t to get it right.

It was to find the edges, and to make sure those edges weren’t already crossed.

Once that felt stable—once it seemed like everything *could* fit without fighting too much—the next step became more deliberate.

Not just “can this exist,” but:

how do you actually make it work?
