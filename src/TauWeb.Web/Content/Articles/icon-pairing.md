## An Icon Two-for-One

In the previous step, I had something that felt like progress.

Words were more solid. Icons had direction. I could look at a card and at least imagine what it might be. Not fully designed, but plausible.

And then the pairing constraint stopped being theoretical.

---

This idea also came from EverDeck, and it’s genuinely clever.

Each icon appears on exactly two cards.

That one decision does a lot of work. It enables matching-style games, memory mechanics, pattern recognition—things that just don’t exist in a normal deck. It’s a great example of getting more gameplay out of something that already has to exist.

But it also means you don’t have 150 icons.

You have 75.

And each one has to work for two different cards.

---

## Adding Suit and Rank Constraints

At first, I didn’t have the additional suit and rank constraints.

Honestly, it would have been much easier without them.

You could just find two words that felt close, give them an icon, and move on. In many cases, the “best” pairings naturally lived near each other—same suit, similar rank, similar tone. It all lined up nicely.

Which is exactly the problem.

---

Because that doesn’t make for very interesting gameplay.

If matching pairs are too close—same suit, same rank, or obvious neighbors—then the match becomes trivial. You’re not really discovering anything, you’re just confirming what’s already obvious.

So I added the rule:

paired cards cannot share the same suit or the same rank.

---

That changes everything.

Now the “easy” pairings are mostly illegal.

Words that feel like they belong together are often sitting in the same suit, or clustered around the same rank. So you can’t just take the obvious answer—you have to work around it.

Which means the pairs get spread out across the deck.

Different suits, different colors, different ranks.

That felt better immediately from a gameplay perspective. Matches aren’t trivial, and the deck has more cross-connections.

It also had a nice side effect visually—paired icons always show up in different suit colors. And by forcing different ranks, one card is always higher than the other. No ties, no ambiguity.

---

## One Picture for Two Words

But it makes the icon problem harder.

Now the question isn’t “what represents this word,” or even “what represents these two words.”

It’s:

what kind of image can work well for two cards that might not be obviously related, and are constrained to live in very different parts of the deck?

---

## Pairing Through Iteration

This is where a lot of iteration happened.

Sometimes the pairing was easy. The two words lined up naturally and suggested a clear image. “Obsidian” and “Shard” both pointing toward broken rock is a good example—there’s a shared idea there, and the icon follows pretty directly.

Other times, the connection was less obvious, but still workable if you thought about it from the right angle. “Guardian” and “Yield” ended up sharing an open portcullis. One reads as protection, the other as passage. Same object, different interpretation.

And sometimes the same object could carry very different meanings depending on how you looked at it. “Death” and “Grain” both using a scythe is a good example. The image is the same, but the context shifts how you interpret it.

That kind of flexibility ended up being more important than I expected.

---

I couldn’t afford to be too strict about matching meanings.

The deck is too varied for that. If I required every pair to align perfectly, I would have been stuck immediately.

But there was still a bar.

Each icon had to make sense on its own, for each card, without relying on the other half of the pair to explain it. If it only worked when you knew both cards, it wasn’t good enough.

---

## Tarot as a Special Case

This is also where Tarot cards became a special case.

For those, I wasn’t just matching the word—I wanted the icon to evoke at least some of the traditional interpretation. Not necessarily the obvious meaning, but something recognizable if you’re familiar with Tarot.

“Empress” is a good example. It’s not just “a ruler” or “a person,” it carries a very specific kind of symbolism that I wanted to hint at, even within these constraints.

That made pairing them a little more delicate.

---

The process here wasn’t clean.

It was a lot of drawing, thinking, trying a pairing, realizing it didn’t quite work, trying another. Sometimes the fix was at the icon level. Sometimes it meant re-pairing the words. And sometimes it meant going all the way back and replacing a word entirely.

Rinse and repeat.

This was probably the most time-consuming part of the whole project.

---

## A Shared Visual World

Along the way, the worldbuilding continued almost by accident.

Not every icon, but certain ideas started to repeat. The farm cycle showed up in a few places. Paths started to connect things. A handful of visual motifs kept reappearing.

None of that was driven by the word system or any formal constraint.

But it made the deck feel more cohesive, like it existed in the same space. It’s the kind of thing you might not notice right away, but it adds up.

---

By the time this stabilized, I had 75 icons, each mapped to exactly two cards, all satisfying the suit and rank constraints, and all at least clearing that minimum bar of “this works.”

Not perfect. But solid.

And importantly, it still felt feasible.

Which brings things back to the question that had been building for a while:

how much of this can you actually show on a card before it starts to fall apart?

## For Players and Designers

For the articles that describe specific systems, I’ll add a short section on how they can be used in gameplay.

Sometimes that’s just mapping to existing mechanics. Sometimes it suggests something a bit different. The goal isn’t to be prescriptive, just to show what naturally falls out of the structure.

Starting with icon pairs.

---

The most obvious use is matching.

Each icon appears exactly twice in the deck, so you can treat them as a built-in set of matches. That supports straightforward memory or matching games without needing to define anything extra.

Because the pairs are constrained to different suits and different ranks, they don’t cluster.

That was intentional. Without that constraint, most of the natural pairs would have ended up near each other—same suit, similar rank—which is easier to design but less interesting to play. Spreading them out forces more movement across the deck and makes matching less predictable.

That also means pairs can interact with other systems.

You might match icons, but still care about suit or rank once you do. Or require that matched pairs satisfy an additional constraint—same sphere, different band, and so on.

Even outside of explicit matching, the duplication creates a subtle echo.

If you see an icon once, you know there is exactly one other copy somewhere else in the deck. That’s enough to build search, memory, or deduction mechanics on top of it.
