## Whoa What is all This Stuff

Early on, I was mostly worried about whether everything could fit.

There was going to be a lot on the card; some stuff was already working, and I knew more was coming (which we’ll get to):

- rank + suit (twice, like a normal deck)
- the word
- the icon
- letter score
- borders + direction
- spheres and modes
- links
- rails
- zodiac

On paper, it all “worked.” Nothing overlapped in a way that was obviously broken.

But that turned out to be the wrong question.

---

## Using the Deck

The real problem shows up the first time you imagine actually using the thing.

Not designing it. Using it.

You shuffle, deal a hand, and look down.

What do you actually see?

---

## Familiar Games

If I’m playing something standard—say I’m mapping to a trick-taking game—I need rank and suit. That’s it.

Everything else should disappear.

The word can’t be too loud. The icon can’t dominate. The borders can’t distract.

Otherwise you end up with something that technically works, but feels like work to read.

---

## Narrative Play

Flip that.

Say I’m doing something narrative instead.

I draw:

- Deluge (R7, red suit)
- icon: flood over a ridge
- mode: Instability
- sphere: Water
- a bunch of other symbols

Now I don’t really care about suit or rank. I care about the word and the icon first, then maybe the mode if I’m digging.

So in that scenario, the corners should fade. The rails should fade. The border triangles definitely shouldn’t be the thing I notice first.

Same card. Completely different priorities.

---

## Borders in Play

This pattern shows up everywhere.

The border system is a good example.

There’s a whole mechanic there—edges, points, matching. It’s usable. It’s interesting.

But most of the time, you’re not using it.

So if every edge is shouting with triangles and structure, even when you’re not playing a border game, it’s just noise.

There’s more here that shows up later. It’s useful—but you won’t need most of it most of the time.

---

## When Information Matters

At some point I stopped thinking about this as “importance” and started thinking about it as “when do I need this.”

That helped.

Because the answer is different for every system:

- **Rank/Suit** → immediate, almost always
- **Word/Icon** → immediate in narrative use, secondary in gameplay
- **Borders** → only when the game cares
- **Spheres/Modes** → situational
- **Rails, partitions, zodiac** → almost always background

---

That shift leads to a more practical goal.

Not “everything should be clear.”

That’s impossible.

The goal is:

if you’re not using a system, it should mostly get out of your way

Not hidden. Just quiet.

---

## Edges and Center

A useful way to think about it is that the card splits into two regions.

The **edges and corners** are functional.The **center** is narrative.

The corners need to behave like a normal deck. You should be able to fan cards, glance at them, and immediately read rank and suit without thinking. That part isn’t negotiable.

The center is where the card becomes something else. That’s where the word lives, where the icon lives, where the more interpretive or thematic elements sit.

Thinking about it that way helped a lot. It gave me permission to treat those areas differently.

---

That doesn’t remove the tension.

The icon wants space—it’s doing a lot of narrative work.The word also wants space—it’s the actual label of the card.Rank/suit need to be instantly readable.

That’s already three things that want to be “first.”

I made peace early on that this was niche, and probably too busy for most people. I did trade some cleverness for clarity. But it still felt worth tuning.

---

## Making the Tradeoffs

So I ended up making a bunch of tradeoffs, iterating, making mockups and adjusting.

My dials were things like size, spacing, fonts, line weights, and color.

Placement mattered just as much as size.

The corners were non-negotiable—they had to behave like a normal deck. If that broke, everything else was irrelevant.

The center belonged to the icon and the word, but even there, they had to share space.

Everything else had to find a place that didn’t interfere. Edges, rails, corners, background—each one needed a consistent home so your eye could learn to ignore it unless you needed it.

---

Shrink the icon slightly so the word can breathe.Keep the corners very standard so gameplay works without thinking.Push the rails into a consistent band so they read as structure, not content.

---

That’s where hierarchy really starts to matter.

Not as a design principle. Just as a practical constraint.

Some things lead — the corners, the icon, the word.Some things support — the glyphs in the other corners.Some things stay quiet unless you go looking for them — borders and rails.

---

I didn’t fully solve that here.

But this is where I stopped pretending everything could be equal.

And once that was clear, the rest of the layout decisions started to fall into place.
