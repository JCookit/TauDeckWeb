## Worth a Thousand Words

With the words getting a little more solid, it was time to start paying attention to the visual side of this.

One of the things EverDeck does really well is anchor each card with an image. The word gives you flexibility, but the image gives you something immediate, provides variety and, as we’ll see, gameplay value also. From the start, I knew the card center belonged to some kind of picture.

But what exactly? There were really two axes here. First was the content. I knew it had to tie to the card word, or else it was just decoration. Was it abstract or concrete? How would a deck’s worth of icons tie together thematically? The other was the design—given the space constraints, how does it balance being simple enough to not dominate, but also feel like it “belongs”?

Also recall that the icons weren’t just adding character (although they need to do that too). They were serving a gameplay function, also borrowed from EverDeck. One of the constraints—something I’ll get into more later—is that each icon appears on exactly two cards to enable a matching-style mechanic. Even at this stage, that was already shaping the choices, even if I wasn’t fully dealing with it yet.

---

## Starting with Symbols

My first instinct was to go more symbolic.

Simple shapes, maybe a little abstract, something clean that you could squint a little and think yeah, that goes with these words. An example was a simple pinwheel-style shape to represent “Wheel”.

That didn’t last very long.

It looked fine on its own, but it didn’t carry enough meaning. You could recognize the shape, but not necessarily connect it back to the word unless you already knew what it was supposed to be. It looked like, well, an icon.

It just didn’t *matter*.And that went against the design principles.

---

## Moving to Scenes

What started to work better was thinking less about symbols and more about scenes.

Not detailed illustrations, and definitely not anything close to realistic drawing, but something grounded enough that you could look at it and at least ask—what is happening here? Is it grounded in something that could be real?

For “Wheel”, I eventually landed on a millstone. A real-world object, something with context and implied action. It’s not the most obvious interpretation, but it’s not disconnected either. And more importantly, it gives you something to react to.

Specific enough to mean something, but flexible enough to be reused.

## Working Within the Visual Budget

This is also where that “visual budget” idea really became concrete.  \[note: this is referring now to a future article; so reword this a little\]

Up to now it was mostly theoretical—yeah, things take space, things need to be readable. Once you start placing icons, you can actually feel it.

If the icon is too small, it loses clarity. If it’s too big, everything else starts getting squeezed out. And there isn’t that much room to negotiate.

This is where “interesting” starts to run into “survivable” in a very real way.

I had already been assuming something like a 1.5-inch square for the icon area, and once you try to fit anything into that space, you realize how quickly it fills up.

I had an early vision for the icon design rules, mostly because of my lack of artistic skill: monoweight, monocolor. But now those ideas had to merge with the actual size and content constraints. I did a few examples and convinced myself this would work to keep the distraction down, still enable some detail, and also match my skill level.

Maybe.

---

## Sketching and Iterating

The process at this stage was coming up with ideas that would match the words and maybe be drawable. Sometimes entire AI conversations happened just brainstorming options. Sometimes it was thumbnail sketches. If things fell apart, I might pair the words differently. If things really fell apart, I went all the way back and substituted different words.

Rinse and repeat.

The result was a list of more solid Words and their Icons, with some sense of feasibility.   Every step helped me feel this was actually possible.

Along the way, there were a couple of “happy accidents,” where seemingly unrelated icons started to feel like they could relate. Maybe these both suggested the same house. Maybe these shared a path. It wasn’t fully fleshed out yet, but the sense that these could belong to the same “world” started to emerge.

That part I wasn’t expecting. But it was a good sign.
