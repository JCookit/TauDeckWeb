## What’s Your Sign?

At this point, I thought I was done with systems.

The deck already had more than enough structure, and I wasn’t actively looking to add anything else.

Then I was at a street fair and saw a few custom 52-card decks. A lot of them had this mystical flair—ornate symbols, decorative glyphs, things that looked interesting but didn’t really do anything.    That was the immediate reaction:  I don’t what that.   But it also got me thinking.

Zodiac and planetary symbols are different.

They’re highly recognizable, even at very small sizes. They already carry meaning, especially for anything loosely in the divination space. And unlike most decorative elements, they have a structure behind them.

So there was an opportunity there.  Not just to add more flavor, but to add something that could **borrow meaning without needing explanation**.

---

## A Partial Overlay

I had also wondered from the beginning, was it worth adding something that worked as a partial overlay.

Up to this point, every system applied to every card. That’s useful, but it’s also very uniform. There’s something different about having a subset of cards that carry an extra property—something that makes them feel a bit more “special.”    So the idea became—can I introduce a subset system that still behaves predictably?

That’s the constraint.

It can’t be random.It can’t break balance.And it can’t require new rules just to understand where things go.

---

## Aligning Signs with Elements

The zodiac signs already come grouped by element.

Fire, earth, air, water—three signs each. That gave a starting point. I could assign zodiac symbols to cards based on their sphere, as long as I stayed within those elemental constraints.

A nice ‘amount’ of these seemed somewhere in the half-deck range – if I placed five of each sign, that would give 60.   To make them seem even more special, I segregated them to the non-face-cards -- ranks 1–10 – after making sure there were the frequence of sphere elements to support this (I wasn’t going to make any sphere-assignment changes now, so there had to be enough available.  3 signs in each element with 5 copies each means 15 of each element sphere had to exist).  Fire signs only appear on fire sphere cards, and so on.

## Planets for the Higher Ranks

One of the reasons for keeping zodiac at the lower ranks was to make the ‘face’ cards feel even more special.   Planets filled that role.    But it didn’t make sense to be random about it there either.    Since there are 6 spheres, it felt appropriate to snap planets to Order and Chaos – and again I had to work within the current sphere assignments.   There were enough in Ranks 11-15 to support two copies of each planet—one in an Order sphere, one in a Chaos sphere.

That keeps them rare and it gives higher ranks something distinct without adding new mechanics.  They become naturally “special” cards, but still fit into everything else.

---

## Finding Room on the Card

At that point, the system worked, but placing it visually was awkward.

The first placement of the zodiac and planet symbols was in the center area, along with the word (reinforcing the narrative/mystical idea).   But this created too much clutter.   Even small, they competed and distracted.

The next logical place was near the sphere and mode cluster in the corner, since they’re related concepts. But that created a different issue -- visual imbalance—even though the glyphs did not appear on every card, it showed up as an asymmetry that bothered me.

---

## A Second Zodiac

The solution was to add a second zodiac.

Not for gameplay reasons, but to restore balance.

That’s one of the few times something in the deck would exist first for visual reasons—but it still needed to earn its place and be a useful system.

Otherwise it really would just be decoration.

---

This is where things got a bit more exploratory.

There are alchemical associations between zodiac signs and processes—things like calcification, dissolution, filtration. It’s not perfectly consistent, but it’s a real thread in the history.

The border system provided a hook.

If you squint a bit, the shape of the borders—fully open, fully enclosed, partially open—can be mapped to those kinds of processes. You have six broad border configurations:

- none
- full
- one side
- three sides
- two parallel
- two adjacent

and twelve zodiac signs --- that was too good a correspondence not to use.

---

So the second zodiac became tied to border configuration. That was enough structure to make it feel intentional:  the shape of a border configuration suggested an alchemical process, and the process suggested a zodiac symbol.   You don’t have to know this to use it in play, but it was important to me that there was underlying meaning.

The target was to apply this to 80 cards also (to match the 60 zodiac + 20 planets of the other system) to create the right kind of rarity. Cards in that set get a zodiac symbol on the left side, near the direction marker, aligned with their border shape.

## Patterns in the Overlap

There are some emergent patterns -- some cards have one zodiac (40 only on the right; 40 only on the left), and 30 have none.    Exactly 40 cards end up with two zodiacs on the card, and exactly 5 of those have two of the same.

Those aren’t required for anything, but hey give you handles if you want to use them.

---

At the end of this, the deck has a mix.

Some cards have no zodiac, some have one, some have two. But across the full set, the visual weight is balanced, and the placements feel intentional rather than decorative.

It’s not a required system.

You can ignore it completely and nothing breaks.

But it does a few useful things:

- introduces a partial overlay
- adds a layer of meaning that doesn’t need explanation
- reinforces the “mathemystical” side of the deck without adding complexity

That’s enough to justify its place.

## For Players and Designers

The most direct use is as a partial overlay.

You can treat zodiac-bearing cards as a special subset—something to collect, avoid, or trigger effects. Because they’re evenly distributed and tied to other systems, they behave predictably.

The elemental alignment also makes them easy to use in combination.

Fire signs only appear on fire sphere cards, so you can build mechanics around matching or contrasting those without needing additional rules.

The planetary symbols work similarly, but with a different emphasis.

Because they are limited to higher ranks and appear only twice each, they naturally act as rare or higher-value elements. That can be used for scoring, objectives, or as special roles in a game.

The dual-zodiac cards create another layer.

Cards with two symbols can be treated as intersections or special cases—either more powerful, more flexible, or restricted depending on the design.

And beyond mechanics, these symbols carry meaning.

If you’re using the deck for interpretation or narrative, the zodiac and planetary associations add another layer without needing to define it. It’s already embedded in the system.
:::
