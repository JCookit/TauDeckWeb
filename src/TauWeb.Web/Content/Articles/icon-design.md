## Cave Paintings

By this point I had a decent set of icon ideas.

Not all solved, but enough that I could see where things were going. The bigger problem was that they didn’t necessarily look like they belonged to the same deck.

Some were simpler. Some had more detail. Some leaned more symbolic, some more scene-based. If I had just kept going like that, it probably would have “worked,” but it wouldn’t have felt cohesive.

And with 150 cards, that starts to matter.

---

## Color as a Constraint

A lot of the constraints here weren’t really stylistic decisions. They were more like guardrails based on what I could actually execute consistently.

The first one was color.

From the previous article, it was already clear that the icon couldn’t dominate the card. It sits in the center, and it’s one of the first things you see. If it’s too loud, it crowds everything else out.

At the same time, I liked the idea that cards should still feel like they belong to their suit.

So there’s a bit of tension there.

I didn’t want full-color illustrations. That would have completely blown out the hierarchy. But I also didn’t want the icon to feel disconnected from the rest of the card.

Where this landed was:

- exclusively black line work
- white fill where needed to separate from the background
- subtle use of suit color as an accent

Once the drawings were working in black and white, I could introduce a bit of color—just enough to tie it to the suit without taking over. It also had a nice side effect with the pairing system. The same icon could appear twice, but pick up a slightly different feel depending on the suit color behind or within it.

## Line Work

The second constraint was line work.

I’m not an artist. Or at least, not a practiced one.

So anything that relied on:

- variable line weight
- shading
- cross-hatching

…was going to fall apart pretty quickly at scale.

A comic-style, monoweight line approach made a lot more sense.

It kept things consistent. It kept things readable. And it meant I could actually execute 75 icons without each one becoming a separate art project.

That said, it wasn’t always easy.

Some objects just don’t translate cleanly into that style. You lose a lot of tools when everything is the same line weight and there’s no shading to lean on. You have to rely on shape and structure more than anything else.   Drawing Night or Thread were particularly challenging without shading.

And there were some things I just avoided entirely.

People and animals, mostly.

Partly because I can’t draw them very well, and the failure there is pretty obvious. A bad rock is fine. A bad tree is probably fine. A bad person or animal sticks out immediately and pulls you out of whatever the card is trying to do.

But it also ended up helping stylistically.

By sticking to objects, environments, and simple scenes, the icons feel a bit more like places or moments rather than illustrations of characters. It keeps them a little more neutral, a little more immersive, and easier to reuse across different interpretations.

---

## Recognizability

Which led to the next focus: recognizability.

A lot of my early attempts were… rough.

Flat shapes, no perspective, things that technically represented the idea but didn’t really read as anything. It had a bit of a “preschool drawing” feel to it.

I knew I could do a little better than that, even if “better” was still pretty basic.

So a lot of the effort went into perspective and structure. Not in a formal sense—just enough that the object feels like it exists in space instead of being pasted onto the card.

That helped more than I expected.

It’s okay if you have to look at something for a second and figure it out. I’m not aiming for instant iconography in every case. But it should get there without too much effort, and without feeling like you’re decoding something completely abstract.

---

## Detail at Card Size

Detail and density turned out to be another real constraint.

Too little detail, and the icon feels empty or ambiguous.Too much, and it turns into noise, especially at card size.

That balance was hard to get right, and it changed depending on the object.

This is where AI ended up being surprisingly useful—not for generating the art, but for critique. I could run through variations quickly, get feedback, adjust, and try again.

Sometimes that meant 10 or 15 iterations on a single icon.

And sometimes it meant giving up and going a different direction entirely. A few of the more abstract or Tarot-driven concepts—World, Outlook, Descent—took multiple passes before landing somewhere that felt usable.

---

## Scenes and Symbols

The “scene vs symbol” idea from earlier held up here.

Pure symbols didn’t carry enough weight. They were easy to draw, but didn’t say much.

Scenes—even very simple ones—gave more to work with. They provided context, supported multiple interpretations, and held up better under the pairing constraint.

So that became the default approach.

---

## Consistency Across the Deck

Putting all of that together, the goal wasn’t to make each icon perfect.

It was to make them consistent.

Consistent enough that when you flip through the deck, nothing feels out of place. Consistent enough that the style disappears and you’re just reading the card instead of noticing how it was drawn.

That’s where things landed.

Not polished illustrations. Not abstract symbols.

Something in between that I could actually execute, 75 times over, without the whole thing drifting apart.

And importantly, something that still fit within the visual constraints of the card.

Because by now, it was clear that even a “good” icon could still break the design if it didn’t respect the space it lived in.
