## I Can Haz Systems?

Now all of the individual systems are in place.

Suits, ranks, icons, links, borders, modes, spheres, partitions, overlays—each one was added for a reason, usually solving a local problem or adding a specific kind of flexibility. None of them were designed as part of a single game, and there isn’t a prescribed way they are supposed to be used together.

But once they are all there, something else happens.

They start to combine.

---

A standard deck already has a version of this.

You get suit and rank, and most games lean on one or both. What this deck does is extend that idea. Instead of two axes, there are many, and they are intentionally independent. You don’t have to use all of them, but you can.

That independence matters.

The systems aren’t layered on top of each other so much as they sit beside each other. You can pick a few, ignore the rest, and things still behave predictably.

---

## Combining Systems

Once you start combining them, a few patterns show up pretty quickly.

Matching is the most obvious.

You can match icons, which gives you exact pairs. You can match suits or colors, which behaves like a standard deck. You can match ranks, or match the last digit of the sequence number, or match letter scores or score tiers. Each of those is technically “matching,” but they don’t feel the same in play. Some are common, some are rare, some are evenly distributed, some are skewed.

Traversal is another.

Word links give you a way to move from card to card without relying on physical position. Ranks can be treated as progression. Spheres or modes can be stepped through in order or used as constraints. None of these require a board, but they still create a sense of movement.

Spatial play is there if you want it.

Borders and direction let you place cards next to each other and care about how they connect. It’s not as rich as a dedicated tile system, but it’s enough to support adjacency, growth, and mapping ideas in a way that normal cards don’t.

---

## Stacking Constraints

Where things get more interesting is when you start stacking constraints.

Instead of “match by suit,” it becomes “same suit, different sphere.” Or “increasing rank, but only within a partition.” Or “follow links, but don’t repeat modes.”

None of those were explicitly designed as rules, but they fall out naturally from the structure. That’s where most of the design space is.

---

## Room for Discovery

There’s also a layer of discovery.

Some of it is obvious, like icon pairs or partitions. Some of it is less so—cycles in the word links, the way border scores distribute, cards that carry two zodiacs. You don’t need any of that to use the deck, but it’s there, and over time it gives you more ways to think about what you’re holding.

---

## A Structured Design Space

If there’s a useful way to think about it, it’s not as a game, and not really as a platform either.

It’s closer to a structured design space.

Each card carries a set of properties, and those properties are consistent across the deck. Games come from choosing which of those properties matter, and how they interact. The deck doesn’t define those interactions, but it makes it unusually easy to construct them.

At this point, it’s useful to step back and look at what actually exists on each card.

Every card carries multiple independent properties. Some are obvious, some are more subtle, but they are all available if needed.

one of 10 suits  (15 each)

## Numeric systems

rank value (1–15)

dual rank encoding via rank rail

letter score (1–6, non-linear distribution)

partition score (1–5, evenly distributed)

border score (0–16, near-linear distribution)

## Relational systems

2 outbound word links

1-5 inbound links (with varying frequency)

icon pairing (exactly one match per card)

## Spatial systems

border configuration (0–4 sides)

border point values per side

direction (compass)

---

Not all of these need to be used at once.   In fact, most games will only use 2–4 of these at a time.

But any of them can be used, and more importantly, they can be combined. That’s where most of the design space comes from.
